Our Philosophy

Carve Your Own Path.

Scroll to Explore

01 /A World of Possibilities

One of the greatest gifts parents hope to give their children through international education is the freedom to see just how many possibilities the world has to offer.

02 /Defining Success for Yourself

Yet much of the tutoring and exam-preparation industry seems to focus on admission to prestigious universities as the ultimate goal, placing academic achievement above all else in pursuit of society's idea of success.

For a long time, I thought the same way—until I began asking myself what success really meant to me.

03 /Letting Curiosity and Ambition Lead

Working hard academically matters, of course. But I believe a student's own interests and aspirations should come first. Once students discover what they genuinely care about, they have a reason to learn—and motivation begins to grow naturally.

As I reflected on what mattered most to me, I found myself returning to tutoring, something I had been doing since high school.

Seeing a student's face light up when something finally makes sense, or hearing from their family long after graduation, gives me a sense of purpose and fulfillment unlike anything else.

04 /A Partner on the Journey

That realization led me to create Camino. The name means "path" in Spanish, reflecting my hope to help students discover their aspirations and find their own way forward.

I aim to be both a mentor who nurtures curiosity and confidence and a tutor who helps students build the academic foundations they need to achieve their goals. Above all, I hope to build lasting, trusting relationships with students and their families.

01

Firsthand International School Experience

Lessons designed around the skills international schools expect—from essays, discussions, and presentations to the challenges students will face as they progress.

Read MoreShow Less

At international schools, strong English and subject knowledge are only part of the picture. Students are also expected to form their own opinions and communicate their ideas through essays, discussions, presentations, and other assignments.

Traditional Japanese cram schools (juku) often focus on the Japanese curriculum and may not fully address how international schools teach or assess students. Having experienced this education firsthand, I design lessons around both the challenges students face today and the skills they'll need in the years ahead.

02

Truly Personalized Tutoring

Every student learns differently. I tailor the subjects, pace, teaching approach, and—wherever possible—the schedule to fit their needs.

Read MoreShow Less

One-on-one tutoring makes it possible to adapt each lesson to the student rather than asking the student to fit a fixed program. That includes everything from how we explain a concept to when we meet.

  • "Please explain things in a way that matches my child's English level, using Japanese when helpful."
  • "Please don't rush through the material. Take time to check that my child understands."
  • "A short break every 30 minutes or so would help."
  • "Usually we'd like to focus on Math, but please prioritize essays when there's an assignment due."

I can adjust the subject matter and teaching style as needs change. I also do my best to accommodate requests for extra lessons before exams or morning sessions on school holidays.

03

Guidance Beyond the Classroom

Connect what your child is learning today with the choices ahead—from universities in Japan and overseas to graduate study and future careers.

Read MoreShow Less

When students focus only on the next assignment or test, it's easy to lose sight of why their studies matter. Understanding the options available to them can help make the work they're doing now feel more meaningful.

Drawing on my international school experience, personal network, university studies, and professional career, I help students explore possible routes through higher education and into the world of work.

04

Support in English and Japanese

In English, Japanese, or a combination of both. Lessons adapt to your child's understanding and language proficiency, helping them move comfortably toward learning in English.

Read MoreShow Less

I teach in both Japanese and English, switching between them according to each student's needs. When understanding an explanation in English is still difficult, we can begin in Japanese and gradually use more English as the student's confidence grows.

Having learned in both languages from childhood, I can also offer practical advice on using Japanese and English in different settings and developing genuine confidence in both.

05

Learning to Think Independently

It starts with a simple question: "Why?" Students learn to ask better questions, organize their thinking, and work logically toward solutions—skills they can use well beyond school.

Read MoreShow Less

Problem-solving matters in almost every part of life. International school students are often asked to raise their own questions, consider different perspectives, and develop reasoned responses. I believe this emphasis on independent thinking is one reason international school graduates can thrive in such a wide range of fields. But these skills aren't always easy to master.

That's why curiosity and structured thinking are central to my lessons. A student's interests and questions can spark a desire to learn, while working through those questions builds the ability to organize ideas and reason step by step. These are learnable skills, not talents you either have or don't have. Drawing on my background in international consulting, I make the frameworks I used at work accessible to children, helping them develop thinking tools they can carry with them throughout life.

My Journey

My Journey

  1. Where It Began

    Discovering International School

    When I was attending a Japanese kindergarten, I joined an international school's summer program. I was drawn to the learning environment and its fresh perspectives, so I enrolled at Marist Brothers International School in Kobe and later transferred to Canadian Academy. Those early experiences sparked my curiosity about the wider world.

  2. Elementary Through High School

    Studying the IB

    I spent my elementary and secondary school years in international schools. In the IB Diploma Programme, I took Math, Physics, Economics, and Japanese at Higher Level (HL), and Language and Literature and Chemistry at Standard Level (SL). Learning alongside classmates from different cultures and backgrounds broadened my view of the world.

  3. London

    Studying Urban Planning at UCL

    I moved to London to study urban planning at UCL. For my dissertation, I worked with a municipal urban planning department in Austria to explore how cities could be made more walkable for women. I also interned at a London architecture firm that supported my research. Along the way, I became increasingly interested not just in designing cities, but in solving the wider problems they face. I wanted to approach those challenges from multiple perspectives, which led me toward consulting.

  4. Travel

    Exploring the World

    As a student and between jobs, I took the opportunity to backpack through Europe, South America, and other regions. I still love visiting new places, exploring different cities, and catching up with friends from my student days.

  5. Career

    Consulting

    For approximately seven years, I worked at European and American strategy consulting firms on projects for governments, local authorities, and major companies in Japan and overseas. Much of my work focused on the future of cities, including autonomous driving and smart city development.

  6. Today

    Bringing Consulting and Education Together at Camino

    Eventually, I returned to an ambition I'd had earlier in life: becoming a teacher. I earned a U.S. teaching certification and began thinking about how to share the skills I'd developed in consulting—asking good questions, thinking logically, and working through complex problems—with young people. I wanted these skills to help students find their own direction, not just improve their grades. That's what led me to found Camino, a name that means "path" in Spanish.
